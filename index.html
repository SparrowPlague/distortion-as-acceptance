Introduction

Research Question

To what extent does interacting with a metaphor-based tangible audio interface facilitate cognitive defusion and reduce the impact of negative self-referential thoughts in participants?

"I am not good enough."

Negative Self-Referential Thought  ·  Example Stimulus

Negative self-referential thoughts — the critical inner voice that says you are not good enough, that you failed, that others see your flaws — are not just fleeting self-doubt. For many people they become persistent patterns that undermine wellbeing and shape how a person moves through the world.

This research asks whether a physical interface — one that makes thoughts audible and distortable through the metaphor of a broken radio — can help create psychological distance from those thoughts. Explore the three lenses through which this question is approached.

Traditional Psychology Thought Suppression

Traditional psychological approaches often rely on suppression — actively trying not to think a thought. Wegner's ironic process theory demonstrates why this fails: monitoring for an unwanted thought paradoxically increases its accessibility.

Cognitive-behavioural approaches challenge the content of negative thoughts through cognitive restructuring. This is effective but treats the thought as a problem to be solved — a different stance than defusion.

Acceptance & Commitment Therapy Cognitive Defusion

Cognitive defusion — a core ACT technique — shifts the relationship with negative thoughts rather than their content. Rather than fighting the thought, defusion creates psychological distance: the thought passes through fully, changed in how it is experienced rather than suppressed.

Research suggests defusion reduces the believability and emotional impact of negative thoughts without necessarily reducing their frequency. This is the key distinction from suppression — and the principle the broken radio interface embodies.

This Research The Broken Radio Interface

Participants recorded a negative self-referential thought in their own voice. A physically modified radio then played that recording back in distorted, modulated form — slowed, pitched, filtered into something strange and unfamiliar.

The goal was not to eliminate the thought, but to make it less literal. Less authoritative. More like a sound passing through broken circuitry than a verdict about who you are. This is cognitive defusion made tangible and audible.

Theoretical Context

Two Ways of Holding
a Difficult Thought

Traditional Psychology

The Suppression
Paradox

Suppression limits the signal. Trying not to think a thought requires continuously monitoring for its presence — and that monitoring keeps the thought active. This is Wegner's ironic process theory (1987): the very act of suppression amplifies what it aims to eliminate.

Do not think of a white bear.

A few seconds is enough to notice. The harder you try not to think it, the more present it becomes. This is suppression working against itself.

Acceptance & Commitment Therapy

Defusion as
Transformation

Defusion transforms the signal rather than clipping it. ACT does not target the truth of the thought — it shifts how you relate to it, loosening its grip without requiring suppression or restructuring.

A defused thought can be held more lightly: "I notice I'm having the thought that I'm not good enough." Same words. Different relationship. Research suggests this reduces the thought's believability and emotional impact — even when the thought itself remains.

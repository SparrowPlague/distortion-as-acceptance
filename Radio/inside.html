Materials & Setup

The materials consisted of a Shure microphone, a MOTU M6 audio interface, a Novation Circuit Rhythm sampler, headphones, and two screens. One screen displayed the session instructions, rendered through a custom Python script. The second screen, connected to the researcher's laptop, displayed the Ableton Live interface used to manage the audio manipulations.

Click on the Ableton logo for the technical specifications & plugins that were used.

Method
Participant and settings

The study was conducted at Leiden University. 18 Participants were recruited through social media, personal networks and university communications. No formal demographic data was collected. Given the sensitive nature of the experiment, participants' privacy was prioritised. As the study was exploratory, the focus was on whether the intervention produced any effect, rather than on demographic differences. Participants were asked to report any hearing impairment or tinnitus. Those with severe hearing loss were excluded, as the task required adequate audio perception. A warning regarding the potential impact of audio manipulations on tinnitus was included in the consent form. The experiment was conducted in both Dutch and English, with participants completing all materials in their preferred language.

A small sample size was anticipated, therefore, a within-subjects design was employed. Dividing participants into an experimental and control group was not feasible, and a pre-post comparison within the same group was considered the most appropriate approach. Participants were exposed to a single experimental condition, namely interaction with the audio interface. Believability, distress and acceptance were measured pre and post experiment. Before the experiment, a CFQ-13 was administered. Post experiment, participants had to complete a questionnaire consisting of Likert-scale items and open-ended questions.

Privacy

To ensure participants' privacy, several measures were taken. The thought remained private throughout the experiment, participants did not need to share their thought (only if they choose to). Prior to recording, the headphone setup was demonstrated using a demo recording to confirm the thought would not be audible to anyone but the participant. During the recording and the interface interaction, the researcher left the room. After the experiment, the audio-file was deleted in front of the participant. As ACT aims to change the functional effects of a thought rather than its content, protecting the privacy was both ethically and theoretically motivated.

Procedure

Each experiment session took approximately 40/50 minutes including consent & debriefing. No fixed time limit was imposed in order to prevent any feeling of performance pressure. There were no sessions that exceeded one hour. Participants were not informed about the theoretical background of the interface or its connection to cognitive defusion techniques. To ensure all participants received identical information prior to the task, instructions were presented in written form rather than verbally. Click to view the instruction text.

Thought selection

Participants were asked to identify one negative self-referential thought that they experienced repeatedly and found personally distressing and believable. To support this process, a worksheet was provided to help participants formulate and articulate their thought. At no point were participants required to share their negative self-referential thought with the researcher, it remained private throughout the experiment. The thought selection was conducted by the researcher, who guided participants through the thought selection process using a standardised set of instructions. A script was written in Dutch and English, so each session the same introduction and explanation was used.

Pre Visual analog scale (VAS)

The impact of the negative self-referential thought was assessed across three outcomes: believability, discomfort and acceptance. This was assessed using a visual analog scale before and after the experiment. Responses ranged from 0 – 100 with 0 (not uncomfortable at all) to 100 (very uncomfortable) for the emotional discomfort, 0 (not believable at all) to 100 (very believable) for the believability scale, and 0 (not accepted at all) to 100 (fully accepted) for the acceptance scale.

Believability and emotional discomfort were selected as outcome measures following Masuda et al. [18][29], who used these dimensions to measure the effect of a cognitive defusion technique. Acceptance was additionally included to examine whether the interface increased participants' comfort with having the thought present, by asking how okay it was for them that the thought was present.

Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire –13

Cognitive fusion was measured prior to the experiment using the Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire [25], A 13-item self-report measure with total scores ranging from 13 to 91, with each item rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (rarely/never) to 7 (always), where higher scores indicate greater cognitive fusion. The experiment aims to facilitate cognitive defusion, therefore, it was relevant to establish the degree to which participants were fused with their thought prior to the experiment. Defusion can be seen as both a technique and a skill. As a technique, it refers to a specific practice or intervention that reduces the impact of thoughts on behaviour and experience without necessarily changing their content [11], which can occur within a single interaction. As a skill, it refers to a more durable ability developed over time and therefore cannot be assessed in a single session. This study addresses cognitive defusion in the former sense, therefore the CFQ-13 was administered only once as a sample characteristic.

Recording of the negative self-referential thought

After completion of the survey, participants were instructed how to record their thought. A Novation Circuit Rhythm sampler was used as the primary control device. Buttons were mapped to record, play and stop. When the participant was confident enough to record their own thought, the researcher left the room and came back as soon as the participant was done recording. Participants could record their thought up to three times and select the recording they preferred.

Graphical user interface

Once participants had finished their recording, the researcher launched the custom graphical user interface (GUI). A screen displayed the GUI, showing a slider for each audio modulation (pitch, robot, reverb, panning and volume). The sliders moved in real time in response to the physical controls on the Novation sampler, providing participants with visual feedback of the active modulations. A legend showing instructions for recording and resetting the audio modulations was positioned in the upper left corner of the screen.

The GUI was intentionally designed to be plain and simple, without too many distractions, to keep participants focused on the audio rather than the screen. Participants were instructed to listen to each audio modulation in isolation before combining them (this instruction was also displayed on screen*), so that they could clearly identify what each modulation sounded like individually. This made it possible to assess the effect of each modulation individually as well as in combination. Once familiar with each effect, participants then freely explored and combined the modulations as they choose. This element of free exploration was deliberate as it reflects ACT’s emphasis on experiential learning. Instead of conversing or imagining, the participants experienced the transformed sound of their own voice in real time. The audio was played in a loop, but participants were able stop the recording if the experience was too uncomfortable or distressing. The researcher walked participants through the interface with the audio on mute, so that the sound of each modulation was encountered for the first time during the experiment.

Post Visual analog scale (VAS) & survey

Following the interaction phase, participants completed a second VAS assessment (same as the VAS prior to the experiment). They also evaluated each modulation separately, rating its effect on believability, emotional discomfort and acceptance as better, neutral or worse. This made it possible to compare the effects of each modulation against each other. The ratings were scaled from -50 to +50. For believability, a higher score indicated the thought felt more believable. For emotional discomfort, a higher score indicated more discomfort. For acceptance, a higher score indicated greater acceptance of the thought.

Analysis of open-ended questions

Open-ended questions were analysed via a qualitative content analysis[30]. Content analysis was chosen for its ability to systematically categorize language through both deductive and inductive approaches, and for its suitability in exploring participants' subjective experiences of the interaction. Coding was conducted by a single researcher using Taguette[31]. To enhance transparency and support replicability, a full codebook including category definitions and illustrative examples was developed and is available here. Categories were refined iteratively during coding, consistent with standard practice in qualitative content analysis [32].

Categories for experiential shifts were derived from theoretical frameworks relevant to cognitive defusion[11].
The following categories were used to code participant responses:

Experiential shifts:

  • Increased intensity: words and phrases describing a stronger experience of the sentence
  • Decreased intensity: words and phrases describing a weaker experience of the sentence
  • Outsider perspective: viewing the sentence from an external position
  • Insider perspective: experiencing the sentence from a first-person viewpoint
  • Deliteralization: the sentence felt less meaningful or less real
  • No shift: explicit indication of no change in experience
  • Descriptive: change described without clear direction

Participant responses were additionally coded for metaphorical language, capturing the figurative expressions used to describe the experience of the audio manipulations. Categories were developed inductively from the data, and where responses contained language corresponding to multiple categories simultaneously, all relevant codes were assigned.

  • Spatial: metaphors of location or distance, describing where the thought was perceived to be
  • Personification: attributing a separate character, voice or intention to the thought
  • Weight: using weight or heaviness to describe the impact of the thought
  • Sound texture: metaphors of tonal quality or texture, reflecting how the modulated audio was heard
  • Atmospheric: metaphors of mood or atmosphere evoked by the modulation, reflecting how the experience felt
  • Repetition: metaphors describing the experience of hearing the thought on repeat

Affective responses were coded based on emotional states explicitly reported by participants, as well as affective states inferred by the researcher from the language used, following the principles of emotion coding as described by Saldaña[32].

The following categories were identified:

  • Discomfort
  • Humor
  • Hurt
  • Loneliness
  • Pleasantness
  • Sadness
  • Seriousness
  • Self-compassion
Closing questions

Two closing questions were asked after completion of all surveys, to capture any remaining reflections and to examine their response to hearing their own recorded voice:

  • Is there anything we didn’t ask, but that you would like to share about your experience?
  • How did you experience hearing your own voice during the experiment?

Technical Specifications

In Ableton[26] an audio effect rack was created, an audio rack is a chain of audio effects/devices that are used for the audio modulation. The chain starts with a compressor for volume control. After the compressor, the graillon 3 (a VST) [27] is used for shifting the pitch (from high to low). The robot modulation was achieved using Swirl, an audio filter native to Ableton that applies a resonant filter effect to the voice. The reverb modulation used Outer Space, a preset that simulates how sound reflects and decays in a large physical space. Utility is used to control the volume, the gain was adjusted to have a minimal db of -20 and a max of 35 db. Participants could not turn the volume down completely, ensuring continuous audio playback throughout the experiment. For the panning of the audio the E4L source panner [28] was used. In this setup played through standard stereo headphones, the perceptible effect resembles conventional left-right panning, but the e4L source panner is designed for 3D (Ambisonic) spatial positioning so there was a subtle spatial depth for the attentive listener. This was chosen deliberately because the ambisonic architecture of the E4L plugin could be a foundation for future work involving more advanced spatial movement of sound. Limiter was used the close the audio chain preventing digital clipping and distortion.

Graillon, Swirl, Outer Space, Utility and the E4L Source panner were mapped via Ableton's MIDI Map Mode to specific knobs on the Novation Circuit Rhythm for real-time manipulation of the modulation parameters during the experiment.

Discussion

The present study explored the use of a tangible audio interface as an approach to facilitating cognitive defusion. Four audio modulations were examined for their effect on the impact of a negative self-referential thought. These were measured through believability, discomfort and acceptance. Given the small sample size, results should be interpreted with caution. The study is exploratory, therefore the results do not confirm a hypothesis. Results rather show an indication of a pattern. Interacting with the interface nonetheless lowered both believability and discomfort overall. Acceptance did not change significantly.

Across the four modulations, robot produced the most consistent results with decreases in believability and increases in acceptance. Reverb had a unexpected result; there was an increase in discomfort instead of a decrease. Panning showed no effect. Pitch produced significant effects on absolute scores, but the raw change scores were not significant and varied widely between participants. These results indicate that the modulations did not produce unidirectional effects. The type of audio modulation and individual differences seem to influence the experience of hearing your own negative thought.

Pitch

Pitch showed significant effects on absolute scores across all three outcome measures, but no significant effects were found on raw change scores. The direction of the responses varied across all three outcome measures. Qualitative data support this, participants reported both increased and decreased intensity in their experience of the sentence, along with a range of affective states. The variety in responses to the pitch modulation could be explained by three factors. First, pitch (also referred to as fundamental frequency, or F0) communicates different emotional qualities depending on its level. Lower pitch has been associated with sadness, while higher pitch has been associated with happiness (among other emotions) [20]. Since participants explored both higher and lower pitch values, some may have found lower pitch more emotionally impactful than other participants. This could be one source of the individual differences observed. A second explanation is that listeners do not all emotionally respond the same way to a given pitch level[20]. Multiple participants reported humour in response to higher pitch, for example, while one participant instead reported seriousness. Third, how representative a recording sounds of someone’s own voice varies from person to person [23]. If a difference in baseline affects how participants respond to a pitch modulation, then this could explain why person a is more affected by high pitch than person b. This is supported by the closing interviews; one participant who identified as non-binary found higher pitch more difficult to hear than lower pitch, while another associated lower pitch with age and higher pitch with youth. Personal identity could be one explanation for why pitch modulations are experienced differently.

Robot

Robot showed the most consistent effects across participants, with significant decreases in believability and increases in acceptance. This finding is unexpected, since robot produced the strongest quantitative effects of the four modulations yet drew fewer specific qualitative descriptions than pitch or reverb. Only two participants reported deliteralization in response to robot [3][11], an effect that reflects cognitive defusion. With pitch, F0 and the formants shifted together, thereby producing an unnaturally high or low voice that still sounded like a distorted version of the participant’s voice. The robot modulation on the other hand altered its timbre. Timbre has been shown to be a primary acoustic cue for vocal emotion perception [19]. If the timbre transformations disrupt the emotional qualities of the voice more effectively, then this could explain why the robot modulation had stronger effects than other modulations. If the voice no longer carries recognizable vocal cues, its function as a negative self-referential statement could therefore weaken. Participants also always included either pitch or robot in their reported combinations, which is consistent with this interpretation, since both modulations alter the vocal qualities most relevant to the emotional impact of the thought.

Reverb

Reverb showed a surprising result; there was a significant increase in discomfort. Believability also increased, although this change was not significant. This is the opposite of the overall pattern, in which the interface lowered both measures. The increase in discomfort could be explained by the longer reverberation time of the used preset. Longer reverberation times strengthen emotional characteristics such as ‘Scary’ and ‘Sad’ in musical instrument sounds [42]. The qualitative data additionally showed that all three atmospheric metaphors used to describe reverb evoked moods on the dark side of the spectrum: ominous, creepy, and haunting. However, another study found that reverberation left unease in music listening unaffected, while enhancing feelings of nostalgia, transcendence and wonder [43]. Although unease and discomfort are distinct measures, they may be related. Both studies used musical stimuli, not spoken material, and unlike theirs, the recorded material in the present study already carried negative emotional content. Reverberation may therefore have intensified how negative the thought already felt, without transforming it. Reverb tends to make a threatening sound feel more threatening, not more transcendent.

Panning

Panning had no effect on believability, discomfort or acceptance, consistent with the qualitative data in which it was most frequently reported as producing no shift in experience. Panning was implemented using a binaural source panner [28]. This is different from a simple stereo left-right effect. A binaural panner creates movement around the listener’s head; however the settings used in this experiment made this effect subtle and barely perceptible through headphones. Binaural audio has been shown to increase emotional impact and engagement in spoken narratives compared to stereo [44]. The implementation used here was relatively simple compared to more advanced binaural or ambisonic techniques, which may explain why no comparable effect was found in this study. Panning also differs from pitch and robot in a more fundamental way. Pitch and robot directly altered the vocal qualities; panning changed only the spatial position of the sound, a property less directly tied to how uncomfortable or believable the thought felt. If panning were extended to a more advanced implementation, it might affect the experience of a negative self-referential thought more strongly.

Overall versus individual

Looking at the overall scores of the interaction we see a decrease in the impact of the thought as a whole even though individual modulations produced varying and sometimes contrasting effects. Several participants showed both increased and decreased intensity within the same interaction, both outsider and insider perspectives, and contrasting affective states. Whether experienced in isolation or in combination, the interface produced a variety of experiences within the same session. There is a downside to this dynamic quality. The increase in discomfort (as seen most clearly with reverb) could carry risks for individuals with higher levels of psychological distress. At this stage of the research, neither the participant nor the researcher can predict which modulation will increase or decrease the impact of the thought. ACT, however, does not aim to reduce distress directly. Instead, the goal is to change the relationship with internal experiences from avoidance to acceptance [3], experiencing uncomfortable thoughts and emotions instead of avoiding them. In that sense, the interface could support this process. One response shows what this could look like:

“Vooral de reverb handeling in combinatie met pitch deed voor mij het meeste. Nog meer merkte ik dat mijn eigen stem met reverb het meeste deed, omdat het als een negatief mantra ging voelen wat een nare priester tegen mij aan het opdreunen was, alleen was ik het zelf. Mogelijk voelde ik daardoor wat meer zelfmedelijden dat ik dit over mezelf denk.”

“The reverb manipulation in combination with pitch had the most impact for me. I noticed even more that my own voice with reverb did the most, because it started to feel like a negative mantra being recited by a dark priest, but it was me. This possibly made me feel some self-compassion for thinking this about myself.”


Psychological flexibility, the ability to experience uncomfortable emotions without them controlling one’s thoughts and actions, is one of the core aims of ACT. Barbosa et al. [38] found that flexibility between immersed and distanced perspectives was associated with adaptive change. Several participants in this experiment showed shifts consistent with this kind of flexibility within a single interaction, moving between increased and decreased intensity, different perspectives, and different affective states. Barbosa et al.’s finding, however, concerns flexibility built gradually over a full course of therapy with a therapist. This differs from the experiment reported here, in which participants interacted with the interface on their own. Whether a single, self-guided interaction can support this kind of flexibility in the same way remains an open question.

A recent study on VR-based cognitive defusion in young people with depression and anxiety [45] compared a VR defusion exercise with a non-immersive audio exercise within a single session. Participants in the audio condition listened to a recording of a therapist guiding them through a cognitive defusion exercise. Although participants preferred VR and rated it as more engaging, the audio condition produced more immediate improvements in several mood-related outcomes. This pattern was attributed by the authors to how demanding cognitive defusion can be. It requires individuals to focus and mentally manipulate a thought, and the audio condition’s lack of visual stimuli may have supported this more effectively than the immersive VR environment. This requirement for focused attention also offers a plausible rationale for the design of the present interface. The interface relied primarily on sound, asking participants to engage directly with the recorded sound of their own negative thought. The screen was kept deliberately plain, showing only the controls and instructions needed to operate it, so that attention stayed on the sound itself. Sound alone may be enough to sustain the kind of attention cognitive defusion requires.

Our results are also consistent with Masuda et al. [18], who found that rapid vocal repetition reduces the believability and discomfort of a negative self-referential thought. There, participants repeated the thought aloud themselves; here, they listened to a looped recording instead. Semantic satiation, caused by sustained exposure to a word’s sound [17], is the mechanism proposed for this effect. This experiment shows that it could extend beyond active repetition. Passive exposure through a looped recording could work in a similar way, although this has not been tested. Even though the interface’s effects fit both studies’ explanations, neither has been tested in this experiment, since no unmodulated audio condition was included. Adding one, in which participants would listen to the recording without any audio modulations, would let two things be tested separately. It would show whether the modulations add anything beyond listening to one’s own voice, and whether passive listening alone produces a reduction similar to that found with active rapid vocal repetition [18]. Comparing the audio condition from the VR study against the modulated audio condition could clarify whether guidance adds anything beyond what the modulated recording alone provides.

Limitations

The most important limitation of this study is its small sample size. With so few participants, the results cannot confirm or reject specific hypotheses about the interface or its individual modulations. They should instead be read as an initial indication of patterns worth testing further, not as confirmatory evidence. A larger sample in future research would help establish whether these patterns hold. A related limitation concerns the absence of a baseline exclusion criterion. Masuda et al. [18] excluded participants whose believability and discomfort scores fell below a set threshold, to ensure the thought used was distressing and believable enough to test. This criterion was not applied here, since doing so would have further reduced an already small sample. As a result, some participants may have started with a thought that did not meet that threshold, which could have weakened the study’s ability to detect real effects. Applying it in later studies would ensure the thought tested is meaningful for every participant. The acceptance measure raises a separate concern. This measure was included to track whether participants grew more accepting of the thought over the course of the interaction. Believability and discomfort were adapted from Masuda et al. [18], while acceptance was developed in the context of this study and has not been validated elsewhere. The lack of a significant change in acceptance could therefore reflect a genuine absence of effect, or it could reflect a limitation of the measure itself. This could be resolved by validating the measure, or using an established one instead.

This study also relied on a single session with no follow-up assessment. Sessions spread over time, together with follow-up assessments, would show whether these effects persist. Repeated sessions carry a risk of their own. The number of times someone hears a recording of their own voice affects how that voice is recognized [41], which could mean participants grow accustomed to it, reducing the interface’s effect over multiple sessions. A single session cannot capture this risk. Future research could also examine whether this effect differs with vocal experience; individuals with extensive vocal training, such as singers, may already relate differently to their own voice.

Participants were free to combine multiple modulations within a single interaction. These combinations were only explored through open-ended questions, not through the same believability, discomfort and acceptance measures used for the individual modulations. It therefore remains unclear whether combining modulations produces something beyond what any single modulation would produce on its own. The open-ended responses were coded through concept analysis, as described in the Methods section. This coding was carried out by a single researcher, so no inter-rater reliability could be established. A second coder analysing the same data, or at least a subset of it, would make it possible to check how consistent these codes are.

Implications

Although this study focused on cognitive defusion, the interface’s underlying goal is helping people become more flexible and adaptive in how they relate to their emotions and thoughts. Other approaches that share this goal could possibly also use similar audio-based tools. Repeated listening to a recording of a negative thought also resembles exposure therapy, where sustained engagement with a distressing stimulus reduces its emotional impact through habituation [47]. This draws on a different underlying process than cognitive defusion. Both, though, encourage engaging with a difficult thought instead of avoiding it.

In practice, the interface could work alongside existing ACT vocal techniques, such as the silly voice exercise [11]. Which technique works best would depend on the client, with a therapist choosing which one works. Participants responded very differently to the individual modulations, which is why that flexibility matters. If no single modulation or vocal technique works the same for everyone, then expanding the range of available tools seems more useful than narrowing it down. The playfulness of the interface may make it easier to sit with an uncomfortable thought. Exploring and combining modulations can feel more like experimentation than direct confrontation. That playful, experimental quality could make it more accessible for people who find abstract, language-based therapeutic exercises hard to engage with. Metaphor comprehension, for example, has been linked to broader language ability [46]. Even when a metaphor is explained clearly, using it still requires the client to actively imagine a scene or concept. Everyone has different imaginative abilities. Hearing the modulated sound of their own voice requires less imaginative effort.

Conclusion

This research explored whether modulating a person’s own recorded voice could support cognitive defusion and reduce the impact of a negative self-referential thought. Interacting with the interface lowered both the believability and discomfort of the thought overall, although the four modulations affected participants differently. Robot showed the most consistent change, and for reverb, discomfort increased instead of decreased. Panning showed no measurable effect. Pitch produced significant effects on absolute scores, but the direction of change varied between participants. To the author’s knowledge, this is the first study to combine cognitive defusion with this kind of voice modulation. With a small sample size, these findings are a starting point for more research. Future work could clarify which modulations work best for which people, and test the interface against an unmodulated baseline recording. This project draws on several fields at once, from voice perception and how sound shapes emotion to negative self-referential thoughts, ACT, and psychology more broadly.

VAS Pre & Post

Prior to analysis, the assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Results indicated that the data did not significantly violate normality for the paired samples analyses (all ps > .05). However, given the small sample size (N = 18), which limits the power of normality tests to detect violations, non-parametric analyses were used as a conservative approach. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted to examine pre- to post-intervention changes across three VAS measures (N = 18). No Bonferroni correction was applied. Results are reported at α = .05 and should be interpreted as indicative rather than confirmatory.

Believability decreased significantly from pre- (M = 82.28, SD = 11.07) to post-intervention (M = 67.11, SD = 24.70), p = .005, r = 0.809, indicating a large effect.

Discomfort decreased significantly from pre- (M = 59.00, SD = 25.08) to post-intervention (M = 49.78, SD = 26.94), p = .017, r = 0.708, indicating a large effect.

No significant change was found for acceptance from pre- (M = 71.78, SD = 21.09) to post-intervention (M = 77.39, SD = 20.74), p = .221, r = −0.367, though the effect size was medium. A negative r value here reflects that post-intervention scores were higher than pre-intervention scores, consistent with the hypothesised increase in acceptance.

Spearman correlations

Spearman correlations were conducted to asses whether cognitive fusion scores (CFQ-13) correlated with change scores on the three VAS measures. No significant correlations were found, suggesting that pre-intervention levels of cognitive fusion did not predict the degree of change in believability, discomfort or acceptance. A significant positive correlation was found between changes in believability and discomfort (rho = 0.491, p = .039), indicating that participants who reported greater reductions in believability also tended to report greater reductions in discomfort. This is consistent with Masuda et al.[29], who identified emotional discomfort and believability as related aspects of the psychological reaction to negative self-referential thoughts.

After using each audio modulation individually, participants then entered a free exploration phase, during which they could combine the audio modulations in any chosen way. Pitch combined with reverb was the most frequently reported combination (n=6), followed by pitch combined with robot and reverb (n=3) and pitch combined with robot (n=3). In every reported combination, either pitch or robot was present. See figure 1.10.

Experiential shifts

All the reported experiential shifts across the two analysed questions are shown in the interactive table. Increased intensity was the most frequently coded shift (n=5), followed by decreased intensity and outsider perspective (both n=4). Insider perspective and no shift were each coded for two participants, and one participant response was coded as descriptive. Pitch, reverb and robot were all three coded across all experiential shifts (Increased & decreased perspective, out & insider perspective and deliteralization).

Several participant responses were both coded with an increase and decrease of intensity. One participant’s response was both coded with outsider and insider perspective. This suggests that the experience and the perspective of the sentence was dynamic rather than unidirectional.

Quote — Increased intensity

“De modulatie van reverb zorgde bij mij voor het meeste ongemak in mijn beleving [omdat het mijn zin versterkte.]”

“The reverb modulation caused me the most discomfort in my experience [because it strengthened my sentence.]”

“De zin werd meer vervaagt, dit zorgde ervoor dat het leek [alsof ik naar andere personen aan het luisteren was.] Dit kwam voornamelijk voor bij de combinatie pitch & robot.”

“Combining pitch, robot and reverb made the sentence [much less of a conviction that can be 'real'. It became more of a 'sound' than a sentence, causing it to lose almost all meaning.]”

Metaphor

Spatial metaphors were most frequently coded (n=6), followed by personification (n=4) and atmospheric metaphors (n=3). Weight, sound texture and repetition were each coded for two participants. Across the two questions, reverb was most frequently mentioned in the context of spatial metaphors (n=5). The combination of pitch and reverb was mentioned three times in context of personification.

Quote — Spatial

“Ja, het vervormen van de stem hoger/lager zorgde er voor dat het niet meer mezelf was die ik hoorde, dit gecombineerd met reverb op hoge stand zorgde ervoor dat klonk alsof iemand in [een andere ruimte] dit aan het zeggen was tegen iemand anders.”

Affective states

Discomfort and humour were most frequently reported (n=4). Pleasantness and seriousness were both reported two times. Followed by hurt, sadness, loneliness and self-compassion (n=1). Across the two questions, pitch was mentioned in the context of humour four times.

No shift

When asked which modulation produced no shift in their experience of the sentence, panning was most frequently reported (n=9). This is consistent with the one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which showed no reduction in believability and discomfort, and no increase of acceptance. Robot was reported two times, volume (n=1) and reverb (n=1). Four participants reported that all modulations produced some form of shift. One participant reported that their experience was largely similar across modulations.

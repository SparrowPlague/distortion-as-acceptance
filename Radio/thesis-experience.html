Negative
Negative self-referential thoughts are evaluative and critical in nature. They judge, diminish, or condemn. Unlike neutral self-observations, they carry emotional weight and that weight is what makes them feel so significant.
Self-referential
These thoughts are directed at the self, not at a situation or an event, but at who you are. They make a claim about your identity: not “I did something wrong” but “I am wrong.” That distinction makes them particularly hard to dismiss.
Automatic
These thoughts arise without intention. You don’t choose to think “I am not good enough” , it appears. This automaticity is what makes them feel like facts rather than opinions: they seem to come from nowhere, which makes them feel true.
Believable
This thought feels true, something you believe. Not like an opinion you could argue with, but like a fact you have to live with. That sense of believability, the feeling that the thought reflects reality, is precisely what gives it its power over you.
Negative Self-Referential Thoughts

Imagine a voice in your mind that tells you you're not good enough, that you failed, that others see your flaws. For many people, negative self-referential thoughts; the critical, evaluative things we say to ourselves, are not just fleeting moments of self-doubt. They can become persistent patterns that undermine wellbeing and influence how a person moves through the world.

Theory & Impact of Negative Self-Referential Thoughts

Negative self-referential thoughts are part of the broader concept of self-talk. Self-talk refers to the internal verbal dialogue we have with ourselves, what is commonly known as our “inner voice.” These self-directed expressions can take many forms: positive or negative, instructional or motivational, and may occur either silently or out loud [1][2]. Self-talk serves a number of functions, including impulse inhibition, regulating courses of action and self-regulation.

Self-referential thoughts specifically refer to thoughts that take the self as their object, that is, thoughts that make claims or evaluations about who one is, rather than about a situation, an event, or another person. Unlike thoughts about external events, negative self-referential thoughts are not merely situational reactions but evaluations of the self as a whole, which may explain why they tend to be more persistent and emotionally significant. Research suggests that negative self-referential processing is associated with a range of psychological difficulties and represents a significant risk factor for the onset and recurrence of depressive episodes [8][9]. Moreover, negative self-referential processing has been linked to a broader disposition toward negative emotionality and distress proneness [10], suggesting that its relevance extends beyond any single clinical category and implicates the individual's broader sense of identity and worth.

It is this specific form of self-talk, negative self-referential statements that are critical, derogatory, or self-defeating in nature, such as “I am not good enough,” “I am worthless,” or “My life is pointless,” that forms the focus of the present research. Understanding why these thoughts are so difficult to disengage from requires a closer look at the psychological process of cognitive fusion, explored in the next section: 'Why does it feel real?'.

Why it feels real

Cognitive fusion is what happens when your thoughts start to feel indistinguishable from reality. Instead of experiencing the world directly, you experience it through the filter of your own mental commentary, your descriptions, judgements and evaluations. The thought "I am not good enough" stops being something you are thinking and starts feeling like something that is simply true. The more fused you are with a thought, the more you believe it.

Theory behind cognitive fusion

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is part of a broader family of cognitive behavioural therapies (CBT). Rather than aiming to reduce or eliminate unwanted thoughts and feelings, ACT seeks to foster a more flexible and open relationship with internal experiences. Unlike traditional cognitive behavioural approaches, which aim to modify the content or reduce the frequency of negative thoughts, ACT does not attempt to change what the inner voice says. Instead, the focus lies on changing the individual's relationship with those thoughts, enabling them to function effectively regardless of what their inner voice is saying [3].

That relationship, however, does not exist in isolation. It always takes place within a psychological and social context. It is precisely this context that determines the degree to which verbal events, such as thoughts and rules, regulate behaviour [3][6]. This insight forms the philosophical foundation of ACT, which is rooted in functional contextualism: a framework that evaluates psychological events not by their form or content, but by their function within a given context. Alongside this, ACT draws on Relational Frame Theory (RFT), a behavioural account of human language and cognition that explains how humans learn to relate events to one another through language, and how these learned verbal relations can come to govern behaviour [7]. Together, these two frameworks explain why thoughts, even when unhelpful or inaccurate, can exert such a strong influence over how a person feels and acts. Private events, including thoughts, sensations and emotions, are therefore not approached by altering their form or frequency, but by targeting their functional effects, that is, the degree to which they govern behaviour, rather than what they say or how often they appear.

Central to ACT is the concept of psychological flexibility, defined as the ability to contact the present moment fully and to persist in or change behaviour in the service of chosen values [5]. Psychological inflexibility, its counterpart, arises when internal experiences such as thoughts, emotions, and memories dominate behaviour in ways that are unhelpful. The ACT model (see figure 1 on the right) identifies six core processes that together contribute to psychological flexibility, each with both a pathological and a therapeutic counterpart, describing both what goes wrong and what can change. Cognitive defusion is one of these six processes, with cognitive fusion representing its pathological counterpart. The process of cognitive defusion will be explained in the next section: ‘What can change?’.

Before turning to defusion, it is important to understand its counterpart. Cognitive fusion refers to the tendency to become so entangled with one's own thoughts that they feel indistinguishable from reality. Rather than recognising a thought as a passing mental event, the individual responds to it as though it is a direct and literal reflection of who they are or how the world is [3][4].

This process is rooted in how language works. Through what is known as verbal processes, the mind constantly generates descriptions, evaluations, and rules about the self and the world [3]. These verbal events, including thoughts such as "I am not good enough," are typically treated as though they correspond directly to reality, a phenomenon referred to as a context of literality. In this context, the mind does not automatically distinguish between a thought and a fact, causing a self-critical statement to stop feeling like an interpretation and start feeling like an objective truth. From a functional contextualist perspective, it is not the thought itself that is harmful, but the context in which it is treated as literal truth and the behavioural regulation that follows from it. The more fused an individual is with a thought, the more they believe it, and the more it governs their emotional experience and behaviour.

Creating distance from the thought

Cognitive defusion is the practice of stepping back from your thoughts rather than getting caught up in them. Instead of treating a thought like "I am not good enough" as a fact you have to live with, defusion invites you to see it for what it is: just a thought. Not a truth, not a command, not a reflection of who you are. The goal is not to get rid of the thought or to think more positively, but simply to change your relationship with it, so that it no longer has to dictate how you feel or what you do.

In practice, this can take many forms; for instance saying "I notice I am having the thought that I am not good enough," to using sound, movement, or metaphor to create distance from the thought.

Cognitive Defusion

Cognitive defusion is conceptualised as altering the literal meaning and behaviour-regulatory function of private events, without necessarily changing their form, frequency, or situational sensitivity [11]. Hayes refers to this process as deliteralisation, learning to hold thoughts less literally rather than eliminating them [3].

This is why ACT places such emphasis on the role of language. According to Relational Frame Theory, verbal relations are bidirectional: the words "I am not good enough" can evoke the same psychological response as if the statement were literally true, simply because the mind has learned to treat the verbal event and the experience as equivalent [12]. ACT does not regard language as inherently problematic, as it serves many important functions in human life. However, ACT takes a pragmatic stance toward the relationship individuals have with language, recognising that the rigid, literal mode in which language is often used can become problematic when private experiences are treated as though they correspond directly to an objective reality. When the same evaluative language continues to govern behaviour without producing meaningful change, defusion offers an alternative: not abandoning language, but relating to it differently.

Simply instructing individuals to take their thoughts less literally is rarely effective on its own. For this reason, ACT places strong emphasis on experiential learning, drawing on metaphors and defusion techniques to create a different relationship with language from the inside out, rather than through analytical instruction alone [13]. The use of metaphor is central to this approach: defusion can be understood as creating a space between the individual and their internal experiences, allowing thoughts to be observed without being acted upon [14].

Defusion techniques take many forms in practice. Some work through language, such as prefacing a thought with "I am having the thought that..." to create linguistic distance. Others rely on visualisation, repetition, or embodied experience to alter the relationship with a thought [11][15]. What these approaches share is an emphasis on experiential rather than analytical change: the goal is not to think differently about a thought, but to experience it differently. From concept to interface

Defusion techniques have one thing in common: they all work by changing how a thought is experienced, rather than what it says. Repeating a word until it loses its meaning, hearing a thought in a different voice, slowing it down or singing it aloud, each of these approaches creates a small but significant shift in the relationship between the person and the thought.

This research brought those principles together in a tangible audio interface, allowing participants to hear and manipulate a recording of their own negative thought in real time. The interface on this website is an illustration of that experience, not a replication of the exact research conditions, but an invitation to explore the same principles yourself.

At the heart of it lies a simple question:
What if interacting with the sound of a thought could facilitate defusion?
